1. Threshold pace and speed advantage: the critical speed model

For all-out efforts lasting about 2 to 15 minutes, the distance you can cover in time t is close to a straight line:

d = CS × t + D′

CS (critical speed, your threshold pace) is the slope: the speed you could keep adding distance at for a long time. D′ (your speed advantage) is the intercept: a fixed amount of distance you can cover faster than CS before you're spent (Monod & Scherrer 1965; in running, Hughson et al. 1984; review: Jones & Vanhatalo 2017).

Dieter fits it from your hardest efforts in the last 26 weeks. It takes a long one L (10–40 min) and a short one S (2–7 min), then solves the two equations:

CS = (dL − dS) ÷ (tL − tS) and D′ = dS − CS × tS

It keeps the answer if it's believable (D′ between 80 and 400 m). Without a usable short effort it assumes a typical trained runner's D′ = {{ e.typical_m }} m and takes CS = (dL − {{ e.typical_m }}) ÷ tL.

2. Race predictions

Rearranging the same line gives the time for a race of distance d:

t = (d − D′) ÷ CS

It's only as good as D′ and is meant for races of about 2 to 30 minutes. With the typical D′, short races (800 m, mile) are the least reliable.

3. Speed load (speed points)

For every moment of a run faster than CS, add up how far ahead of a threshold-pace runner you got:

load = Σ (v − CS) × Δt, over all moments where v > CS

It's measured in metres: the same units as D′. This is the distance version of the "W′ used above critical power" idea (Jones & Vanhatalo 2017). It doesn't subtract recovery: it's the total running above threshold. Dieter computes it from each run's second-by-second speeds, grouped into 0.05 m/s bands.

4. Speed fitness and this week's status

speed fitness = average weekly speed load over the {{ e.chronic_weeks }} weeks before this one

ratio = this week's speed load ÷ speed fitness: under {{ e.easing }} easing off, up to {{ e.holding }} holding, up to {{ e.ratio }} building.

5. Safe limits

speed load limit = max({{ e.ratio }} × speed fitness, {{ e.floor }} × D′)

This is the acute:chronic workload ratio: injury rates rose in team-sport data when a week's load went past about 1.5 times the recent average (Gabbett 2016). It's debated: the ratio has statistical problems and hasn't been shown to cause injuries (Impellizzeri et al. 2020), so treat it as a guide, not a rule. Dieter uses {{ e.ratio }} rather than 1.5, set to your own training: your week of Nov 23, 2026 sits just under the limit, and the 18 × 400 week before it is still well over. "Well over" starts at {{ e.well_over }} × the limit.

near-sprint metres = Σ distance run at v ≥ 0.9 × vmax, where vmax is your fastest 5 seconds in 26 weeks{% if e.vmax_pace %} ({{ e.vmax_pace }}/mi, so near-sprint starts at {{ e.near_top_pace }}/mi){% endif %}. Its limit is {{ e.ratio }} × the 4-week average, never below 500 m.
